Relay · visual direction, second pass, with your answers · 2026-11-20

How Relay should look

You've answered all seven questions. The composition below puts your answers together, in both themes, with the accent-tinted grays you chose next to plain gray so you can settle question 3 on the final palette. What's left is your final approval of the whole direction. Nothing has been built yet: the colors, type, shapes, and spacing are rebuilt once you approve, and then the components.

1. Direction summary

What Relay's screens must do

  • The job that needs action now (unassigned, or about to run late) is the first thing a dispatcher sees.
  • Status is the main signal, so the status colors are the strongest color in any list of jobs.
  • Dispatchers follow long rows all day, so rows must be easy to track without heavy lines, and quiet enough for an eight-hour shift.
  • Job numbers, times, and names must be read correctly at small sizes, at a glance.
  • It should feel like a current, well-made tool with its own character, not a generic admin template.

Your answers

  • Color: Quiet. The accent only on the main button, checkboxes, focus, links, and selection; the current page in the sidebar is gray.
  • Accent: Violet 550 from the palette library, #7B43E6.
  • Grays: Tinted toward the accent, to confirm against plain gray in section 3.
  • Corners: 8px on buttons and fields, panels 12px; status chips with a small radius (5px), not pills.
  • Panels: Lifted with a soft shadow, no outlines; lines only where they help you click, type, or follow a row.
  • Type: Atkinson Hyperlegible Next throughout.
  • Density and theme stay as recorded: dense, light and dark (light first).

What Relay won't look like

  • A grid of outlined boxes: no outline on panels, chips, tooltips, or menus.
  • A dated office app: flat gray boxes, hard edges, and lines everywhere.
  • A soft consumer app with big rounded cards and pastel color.

In two sentences: Relay is a quiet, near-neutral work surface where panels sit lightly lifted on soft shadows, the job that needs a dispatcher right now is lifted further, and status color does the shouting. Violet appears only where you act or have selected something, and lines only where you click, type, or follow a row, all set in one highly legible typeface.

1How much color?

How far the accent reaches beyond buttons and focus.

Your answer: A, Quiet (I had proposed Brand.)

Why I proposed Brand

Status has to be the strongest signal in the table, which rules out Tinted for an all-day tool. Quiet is what Relay had, and with gray everywhere it read as a generic office app. Brand gives Relay a recognizable color in the places that mean "you are here" and "you picked this", and leaves the table to status.

2Which accent color?

Petrol is out. These are the most saturated middle stops of three colorful palettes in Sistema's palette library, chosen to stay apart from the status colors: green (on site, done, hue about 149), amber (running late, about 58), red (parts hold, about 27), and blue (en route, about 263). Because you approved grays tinted toward the accent, each option's grays follow its accent.

Your answer: C, Violet 550 · #7B43E6 (I had proposed Purple 550 · #8E38DF.)

Why I proposed Purple 550 · #8E38DF

Status must stay the strongest, clearest signal in the table, so the accent has to be a color nobody mistakes for a status. Purple is far enough from the blue "En route" chip that its selected rows and highlighted page never read as blue, and it is less shouty than fuchsia on a screen dispatchers look at for eight hours. It matches Relay's energetic, positive voice without the "every startup is violet" look. Fuchsia is the bolder choice if you want Relay to stand out more; violet is the closest to Linear.

7Which typeface?

One typeface for everything: titles, table rows, labels. Each option sets the same screen.

Your answer: C, Atkinson Hyperlegible Next (I had proposed IBM Plex Sans.)

Why I proposed IBM Plex Sans

Job numbers, times, and tech initials must be read correctly at 13px all day, which favors clear, distinct figures. Plex has that and a workmanlike character that suits a trades business; Inter is the safe neutral choice. No second typeface: tabular figures handle job numbers and times, so a monospace font is not needed yet.

3. The composition

All seven answers together, in both themes. On the left, the grays tinted toward violet (your answer); on the right, the same page with plain gray, everything else identical, so you can confirm question 3. The content is placeholder content arranged by role: one featured job, panels on soft shadows, a violet brand block, an inverse toast. The real hierarchy is settled once there are real screens.

Light (primary)

Dark

The difference is small on purpose: the tinted grays carry a faint violet (OKLCH chroma about 0.017 at hue 294), the plain ones almost none (about 0.006; the specimen's plain gray is not perfectly neutral). It shows most in the page background, the sidebar, the borders, and the dark theme's surfaces. Both pass every text contrast check.

What every line on it is for

  • Search field, Export button, "Tech: All" filter, checkboxes: the edge shows where to click or type.
  • Thin rules between table rows: they help the eye follow a seven-column row across the screen.
  • The bar under the job steps: it is the progress indicator itself.
  • Nothing else has an outline. Panels lift on a soft shadow; the featured job, the menu, and the toast lift on stronger ones. In dark, raised surfaces also get lighter, because shadow alone barely shows on a dark page.

Squint check: the violet "Jobs done today" block and the featured job read first, then the status chips and the "New job" button, then the table text; the sidebar reads last. With Quiet color the brand block is the only large area of violet, which is why the emphasis map keeps it off the board, so the featured job leads.
